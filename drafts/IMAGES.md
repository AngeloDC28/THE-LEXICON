# Image sourcing for the three pieces

Three different rights situations. Handle them differently.

---

## Piece 2 — Marie Antoinette — FREE AND CLEAR

The only piece where you can publish high-resolution images with no permission and no risk. Both portraits are from 1783 and the painter died in 1842, so they are unambiguously public domain worldwide.

**IMAGE 1 — Vigée Le Brun, *Marie Antoinette in a Chemise Dress* (1783)**
The scandal picture. Oil on canvas, 89.8 × 72 cm, Hessische Hausstiftung, Kronberg.

- Scholarly page with full analysis: https://fashionhistory.fitnyc.edu/1783-vigee-le-brun-antoinette-chemise/
- Wikimedia Commons — search "Marie Antoinette in a Chemise Dress" for the highest-resolution file
- Also viewable through the Metropolitan Museum collection database

**IMAGE 2 — Vigée Le Brun, *Marie Antoinette with a Rose* (1783)**
The replacement portrait after the chemise picture was withdrawn from the Salon. Showing them side by side is the whole argument in two images.

- https://fashionhistory.fitnyc.edu/1783-vigee-le-brun-antoinette/
- Wikimedia Commons for the file

**IMAGE 3 — Exhibition installation view**
Copyrighted. Request from the **Château de Versailles press office**, which issues press images for exhibitions as a matter of course.

Do this. It costs one email, it usually works, and it gets you a press contact at a major national institution — which is worth as much as the image. Ask for press images for *Marie-Antoinette by Sofia Coppola*, say where you're publishing, and give the publication date.

> Use the FIT Fashion History Timeline as a citation throughout, not just an image source. It's a peer-reviewed fashion-history resource and citing it signals you know the field.

## Pieces 1 and 3 — film stills — FAIR DEALING

Film stills are copyrighted. You are relying on the UK exception at **s.30(1), Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988** — fair dealing for criticism and review.

Three conditions, all of which you meet if you're careful:

1. **The use must genuinely be criticism or review.** These pieces are. Don't use an image purely decoratively.
2. **Sufficient acknowledgement.** Title, director, year, production company, in the caption. Every time.
3. **Fair.** Use as few as the argument requires, at web resolution, not full-bleed hero images.

This is a real and well-established exception, not a loophole. Publishers use it routinely. But keep the images small, captioned and load-bearing.

### Piece 1 — Lynch

All nine campaigns are on YouTube. Screenshot what you need.

- **Blue rose in the Dior handbag**, *Lady Blue Shanghai* (2010) — the closing image, and the essay's central claim
- **Black panther**, Jil Sander *The Instinct of Life* (1993) — pair it with the *Lost Highway* opening if you want to make the laboratory argument visually
- **Heather Graham**, Calvin Klein Obsession (1988) — evidence for the "best of the sequence" verdict
- **The YouTube search results page itself**, screenshotted, for the archive section

That last one is the strongest image in the piece and it's free of any rights issue. A screenshot of inconsistent titles, mismatched lengths and wrong dates *is* the argument about preservation. Do not skip it.

### Piece 3 — Duke of Burgundy

- **The title card reading "Perfume by Je Suis Gizella"** — essential. The entire essay is about this frame. Pause the opening credits and capture it.
- **A production still** of Knudsen and D'Anna — request from **Artificial Eye / Curzon**, the UK distributor, or check the BFI's film page: https://www.bfi.org.uk/film/37a7855a-1183-5b8a-827b-5e2631ab699d/the-duke-of-burgundy

Film4 co-produced it, so Film4's press office is a second route.

## Piece 4 — Mean Girls — FAIR DEALING

Paramount Pictures, 2004. Same s.30 conditions.

- **The Plastics in the cafeteria** — the uniform, in one frame
- **Cady in the oversized pink polo** — the "borrowed from Damian" costume, which is the piece's best single observation

Two images is plenty. Resist using more; this film is over-illustrated everywhere on the internet and restraint will make the page look edited rather than aggregated.

## Piece 5 — The Cook, the Thief — FAIR DEALING

Palace Pictures, 1989. Same s.30 conditions.

The colour rule only works visually if you show it, so this is the one piece where you need at least two images and they must be a matched pair:

- **Georgina in the red dining room**
- **The green kitchen**

Same character, different room, different colour. Side by side, captioned so the reader sees the change. That pairing is the entire argument and no amount of prose substitutes for it.

Film-Grab has a comprehensive frame gallery for this title, which is useful for *finding* the exact frames you want — then capture them yourself from your own copy for the published piece.

A third image of the crimson waiter uniforms is worth it if the layout allows.
