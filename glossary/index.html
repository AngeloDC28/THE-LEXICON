Glossary

The words, explained.

Start with the fashion basics, then the ideas. Each definition gives the plain meaning first, then the thinker it comes from. In any show's notes, tap an underlined word for its definition.

Fashion basics

archive
A brand's or museum's store of past clothes, photos and documents. THE LEXICON is an archive of shows, explained.
collection
The set of outfits a designer makes for one season, shown together at once.
couture
Short for haute couture: one-off clothes made by hand, to measure, for a single client, by houses that meet the official Paris rules.
creative director
The designer in charge of what a house makes each season. Houses change creative directors: Jonathan Anderson at Loewe, Alessandro Michele at Gucci.
fashion week
A week of shows held in one city each season. The big four are New York, London, Milan and Paris.
house
A fashion company, usually named after its founder (the house of Dior, the house of Chanel). The house keeps going after the founder leaves.
look
One complete outfit as it appears in a show, from head to toe. Shows are counted in looks: "look 1" opens the show.
menswear / womenswear
Collections made for men or for women. Many designers now show both together, or make clothes for anyone.
ready-to-wear
Clothes made in standard sizes to be sold in shops. Most designer collections are ready-to-wear.
runway
The walkway models walk down at a show (also called the catwalk).
show
The event where a collection is presented, usually on a runway in front of press and buyers. Some shows are films, installations or performances instead.
SS / AW
Spring/Summer and Autumn/Winter, the two main fashion seasons. Collections are shown about six months early: an SS 1999 show took place in autumn 1998.

Ideas and theory

A B C D G H I L M P Q R S T W Z

abjection
Julia Kristeva's concept: the things we find repulsive — dirt, bodily fluids, death — that we push away to feel "clean" and human. Fashion that engages with abjection deliberately unsettles this boundary.
appropriation
Borrowing cultural elements from another group — especially when the borrowing group has more power than the source. A charged and debated concept in fashion, from kimonos on runways to cornrows on catwalks.
avant-garde
Ahead of its time — challenging existing norms and pushing into new territory. In fashion: work that's experimental, difficult, or commercially risky. The cutting edge.
body politics
The political dimension of the human body — who controls it, how it's displayed, whose bodies are considered "acceptable" or "deviant." Fashion is a primary site where body politics play out.
bricolage
Making something new by assembling existing bits — a patchwork of references, materials, or styles. Punk is bricolage. Vetements is bricolage. The art of making meaning from fragments.
camp
Susan Sontag's concept: an aesthetic that delights in artifice, exaggeration, and bad taste used well. Camp is theatrical, ironic, and knows it's over the top.
cyborg
A hybrid of human and machine (from Donna Haraway's Cyborg Manifesto). In fashion: garments that extend, replace, or question where the human body ends and technology begins.
deconstruction
Taken apart on purpose. A method of design (borrowed from philosophy) that exposes a garment's seams, structure, and construction instead of hiding them. The "unfinished" look is a deliberate statement.
deconstructionism
The theory behind deconstruction — questioning whether anything has a fixed or "correct" meaning. In fashion, it means dismantling the rules of what clothes should look like or how they should be made.
drape
How fabric falls from the body — the quality of cloth in movement and at rest. Drape is about gravity, material, and the relationship between cloth and human form.
grotesque
Distorted, monstrous, excessive — the aesthetic of excess and transgression. Fashion grotesque deliberately makes the beautiful ugly, or the ugly beautiful.
habitus
Pierre Bourdieu's word for the unconscious habits, tastes, and ways of moving through the world that are shaped by your class and upbringing. Your "feel for the game" of society — including fashion.
hauntology
Mark Fisher's concept: being haunted by the futures that never happened. Nostalgia for a past that wasn't, or grief for a future we didn't get. Raf Simons' rave-era references are hauntological.
haute couture
Literally "high sewing." Custom-made fashion from Paris's elite ateliers, governed by strict regulations. Made for individual clients, often as much sculpture as clothing.
hegemony
The way dominant groups maintain power not through force but through culture — making their values seem natural and universal. Fashion's beauty standards are a form of hegemony.
heteronormativity
The assumption that heterosexuality is the default, natural state. Fashion that challenges gender norms or queers the body is pushing back against heteronormative codes.
hybridity
Homi Bhabha's term for cultural mixing — the "third space" created when two cultures meet. In fashion: garments that fuse traditions, aesthetics, or references from multiple cultural sources.
institutional critique
Art (or fashion) that questions or satirises the institutions it belongs to — galleries, fashion houses, luxury brands. Turning the tools of the system against the system itself.
intersectionality
Kimberlé Crenshaw's framework: our identities (race, gender, class, disability, sexuality) aren't separate — they intersect and shape each other. Fashion can reinforce or challenge these overlapping systems.
liminality
Being in-between — on the threshold of two states. A garment that's not quite one gender, era, or form is in a liminal space. Transitional, ambiguous, unresolved.
lineage
The chain of influence — where an idea, silhouette, or technique came from and what it went on to influence. The Lexicon maps fashion's lineage.
materiality
The physical, material reality of things — fabric, texture, weight, construction. A theory that takes the "stuff" of clothing seriously, not just its symbolic meaning.
performativity
The idea that identity is performed, not innate. We become who we are through repeated actions — including the way we dress. You don't dress to express who you are; you dress to become who you are.
phenomenology
A philosophy focused on lived experience — how the body perceives and moves through the world. In fashion: how does it feel to wear this? How does it change how you move and how others perceive you?
post-colonialism
The study of how colonialism's effects continue after formal empires end — in culture, language, fashion, and identity. Who gets to claim which aesthetic traditions?
post-humanism
The idea that humans and technology are merging — that our bodies, identities, and futures are shaped by machines, prosthetics, and digital systems. In fashion: clothes that blur the line between skin and machine.
prêt-à-porter
French for "ready-to-wear." Clothes made in standardised sizes for mass production, as opposed to bespoke or couture pieces made for a single person.
provenance
Where something comes from — its history, origins, and lineage. In fashion criticism: tracing which earlier garments, designers, or movements inspired a particular look.
queer performativity
The idea (from Judith Butler) that gender isn't something you are — it's something you perform, through repeated acts and styles. Fashion is one of the most visible arenas for this performance.
ready-to-wear
The same as prêt-à-porter — mass-produced fashion made in standard sizes. The category that most fashion sits in, from high street to luxury.
semiotic
Relating to semiotics — the study of signs and symbols. Semiotics in fashion asks: what does this garment signify? A white dress signals purity in one context, mourning in another.
silhouette
The overall shape of a garment against the body. The triangle of a 1950s full skirt. The rectangle of 1990s minimalism. Silhouette is fashion's most legible grammar.
simulacrum
Baudrillard's term for a copy with no original. In the age of mass production and social media, are we wearing copies of copies? Is "authentic" fashion even possible?
structuralism
A way of understanding culture by looking at underlying systems and rules — like grammar in a language. Applied to fashion: the idea that clothing creates meaning through contrast (formal/casual, covered/exposed).
subcultural resistance
Using style and dress to push back against mainstream culture. From punk to ballroom, subcultures build visual codes that signal belonging and opposition at the same time.
surrealism
An art movement (1920s onward) that draws on dreams, the unconscious, and the bizarre. Fashion surrealism places strange objects (a shoe as a hat, a skeleton as a corset) in contact with the body.
symbolic exchange
Jean Baudrillard's idea that in consumer culture, objects are valued for their symbolic meaning rather than their use. A logo handbag isn't about the bag — it's about what the logo signals.
the uncanny
Freud's term: something that is eerily familiar and strange at the same time. A mannequin that almost looks human. A dress that mimics a body. Fashion that unsettles by being almost-right.
wabi-sabi
The Japanese aesthetic of finding beauty in imperfection, incompleteness, and transience. Cracked pottery. Faded fabric. The anti-perfection of Yamamoto.
zeitgeist
The spirit or mood of a particular time — the invisible atmosphere of an era that shapes everything produced in it. A great designer captures the zeitgeist; a bad one chases it too late.
