01 PROVENANCE ~3 MIN

Where the gesture came from.

The 13th collection McQueen showed under his own name closed with a tableau that has, in the twenty-five years since, been reproduced more often than almost any other moment in late twentieth-century fashion. The Gainsborough Studios in Shoreditch, an audience seated on bleachers, and Shalom Harlow standing on a rotating wooden disc in a strapless white cotton dress.¹ Two industrial robotic arms — leased from a Fiat factory in Turin — flanked her, and as the disc began to turn, they began to spray.

The lineage is precise but rarely traced. McQueen's reference for the gesture was Rebecca Horn's High Moon (1991) — two rifles, suspended at face height, periodically firing red paint at each other across the gallery.² Horn had spent the 1970s building wearable extension prosthetics, body-mounted apparatus that performed actions on the wearer. McQueen substituted automotive industry for studio practice, and the audience for the artist.

The dress is not the work. The dress is the witness. — McQueen, in conversation with Susannah Frankel, 2003
"Authorship is delegated to the apparatus; the body becomes site, not subject — a transfer of agency that the next twenty years of fashion would attempt, mostly unsuccessfully, to repeat."
02 CRITIQUE ~6 MIN

What the gesture argued.

To read No. 13 as a "spectacle" — the term most often applied to it in fashion press of the period — is to miss the structural argument. McQueen's intervention was not theatrical but procedural: he refused the conventional choreography in which the designer authors the garment and the model presents it.³ Authorship was delegated to the apparatus; the body became site rather than subject.

This is the move that distinguishes the work from its many subsequent imitators. The garments Rei Kawakubo would later produce under conditions of similar process-driven authorship — the 1997 "Lumps and Bumps" collection, in particular — share the analytical framework but diverge in execution. Where Kawakubo defamiliarises the body through the silhouette of the garment itself, McQueen defamiliarises the act of making the garment by inserting the audience into its final composition.⁴

What the gesture produced.
