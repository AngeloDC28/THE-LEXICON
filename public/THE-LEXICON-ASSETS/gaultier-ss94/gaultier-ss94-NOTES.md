# gaultier-ss94
## Jean Paul Gaultier SS 1994 — "Les Tatouages"

### Collection Overview
- **Designer:** Jean Paul Gaultier
- **Season:** Spring/Summer 1994
- **Show Date:** October 18, 1993
- **Venue:** Galerie Vivienne, Paris
- **Show Title:** "Les Tatouages" (The Tattoos)

### Curatorial Notes
"Les Tatouages" is widely regarded as Jean Paul Gaultier's defining collection — the crystallisation of every obsession that had animated his career to that point — and was selected by Vogue as one of the 25 most unforgettable shows of the 1990s. Presented in the Galerie Vivienne, a grand 19th-century Parisian arcade that also housed Gaultier's flagship store, the show comprised nearly 100 looks and unfolded in an atmosphere thick with incense and drifting, mystical music.

The collection was a maximalist counterpoint to the monochrome severity of the preceding season's "Chic Rabbis." Where AW 1993 had drawn from a single religious visual system and worked in tonal restraint, SS 1994 exploded outward in every direction: Indian mysticism, 18th-century silhouettes, 13th-century martyrdom, haute couture construction, punk, fetish wear, denim workwear, and — at its core — the tattooed and adorned body as both canvas and garment. Gaultier's method was a picaresque collage of time and place, fusing disparate cultural sign systems into looks that could not be reduced to any single source.

The collection's central conceit was the confusion between skin and fabric. Gaultier had been attending tattoo conventions and had encountered the transgressive work of Los Angeles–based performance artist Ron Athey. He spoke of seeing an older woman in a sheer blouse whose bra turned out to be a tattoo — a moment of perceptual slippage that became the leitmotif of the collection. Trompe l'oeil tattoo prints on second-skin mesh created the illusion of inked bodies; actual tattoos on models blurred the line further. Designs were also stencilled directly onto the models' skin. The tattoo-currency motif — intricate prints replicating the patterns of European banknotes — echoed a technique Martin Margiela, Gaultier's former protégé, had pioneered in his debut collection of 1989.

The casting was as radical as the clothes. Gaultier placed the definitive supermodels of the era — Naomi Campbell, Christy Turlington, Nadja Auermann, Yasmeen Ghauri, Tatjana Patitz, Kirsten Owen — alongside individuals drawn from his personal orbit and from the street: Rossy de Palma (the Almodóvar favourite), Ève Salvail (with her shaved head and dragon tattoo), Jenny Shimizu, Stella Tennant (in one of her first Paris appearances, rendered almost unrecognisable), Vladimir McCrary, Tanel Bedrossiantz (who would become Gaultier's male muse for over two decades), and Laetitia Casta. Men walked in skirts and sarongs. The spectrum of race, gender, age, and body type on display was instinctive but deliberate — a statement about the "global village" street culture of Paris that Gaultier cited as his primary inspiration.

The finale remains one of the most iconic sequences in fashion show history: shimmering saints in heavenly crowns, followed by models with one arm sheathed in medieval armour, evoking Joan of Arc — the most iconic French saint — in a fusion of chainmail replicated in silver sequins, fine metallic knitwear, loops of metal, and actual armour panels worn over deconstructed corset dresses.

The collection is significant on multiple levels: as the apotheosis of Gaultier's postmodern semiotic method; as a landmark in the representation of diversity on a high-fashion runway; as a document of early-1990s cross-cultural aesthetics and their attendant tensions around appropriation; and as the origin point for the tattoo-print mesh garment — a piece that has become one of the most recognisable and widely imitated fashion archetypes of the past three decades. It is essential to any archive concerned with the intersection of fashion, the body, subcultural identity, and the politics of representation.

### Target Looks 01–05

**Look 01**
The opening salvo establishes the collection's tonal register: the body as site of adornment. Expect a trompe l'oeil tattoo-print mesh top — second-skin sheer fabric printed with tribal or ornamental tattoo motifs — paired with a draped or wrapped lower half (sarong, kilt, or low-slung trouser). Face jewellery, nose-to-ear chains, and piles of beads signal the cross-cultural programme immediately. The model may carry a joss stick. The look reads as punk-meets-Eastern-mystic: pierced, inked, adorned, and deliberately ambiguous in its gender coding.

**Look 02**
The second look develops the denim strand of the collection. Gaultier cut denim into 18th-century silhouettes — a full-skirted coat or jacket with the proportional vocabulary of Christian Dior's New Look, executed in patchwork denim. The collision of workwear material and haute couture form is the point: the garment is technically accomplished (Gaultier's tailoring in this period was peerless) but aggressively demotic in material. Possibly paired with a corset visible at the bodice.

**Look 03**
Introduction of the currency-print motif. Skin-tight opaque or semi-sheer fabrics printed with the intricate background patterns of European paper money — denominations, filigree, micro-text — creating garments that function as a commentary on the body's commodification. The print is dense, almost hallucinatory in its detail. Gaultier may combine the currency print with a corset or bustier construction, layering the semiotic registers of sex, commerce, and craft.

**Look 04**
A look that foregrounds the Joan of Arc / medieval armour strand. Chainmail — replicated in silver sequins, fine metallic knitwear, or metal loops — worn over or integrated with a deconstructed corset dress or gown. One arm may be sheathed in an articulated armour panel. The look fuses masculine (warrior, knight) and feminine (saint, bride, corseted figure) in a single silhouette, evoking the androgynous heroism of Joan of Arc. This strand intensified toward the finale but was threaded through the collection.

**Look 05**
A male look that crystallises the collection's gender subversion. A man in a kilt or sarong — Gaultier's signature provocation — with a tattoo-print mesh top revealing or simulating a tattooed torso. The styling is heavy with jewellery, piercings, and beads. The look may feature one of the collection's male muses (Vladimir McCrary or Tanel Bedrossiantz). The effect is neither drag nor conventional menswear but a third register: the adorned male body as an object of beauty and cultural hybridity, presented without irony or apology.
