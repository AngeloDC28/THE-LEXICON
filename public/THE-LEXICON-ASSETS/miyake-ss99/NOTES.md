# miyake-ss99
## Issey Miyake SS 1999 — "A-POC" (A Piece of Cloth)

### Collection Overview
- **Designer:** Issey Miyake (with Dai Fujiwara as co-creator of the A-POC concept)
- **Season:** Spring/Summer 1999
- **Show Date:** October 1998
- **Venue:** Paris
- **Show Title / Concept:** "A-POC" (A Piece of Cloth)

### Curatorial Notes
"A-POC" (A Piece of Cloth) was not merely a collection but the public introduction of a new manufacturing philosophy — one that Miyake and engineer/designer Dai Fujiwara had been developing for several years. The concept proposed that a complete garment, including seams, pockets, and structural elements, could be produced in a single continuous industrial knitting process, eliminating cutting and sewing waste entirely. Each garment was contained within a tube of fabric; the wearer activated it by cutting along pre-knitted lines to release sleeves, necklines, and hemlines.

The show made this manufacturing logic into its theatrical premise. Models walked the runway physically connected — the red knit tube ran continuously between them, linking bodies in a single unbroken textile system. The image of multiple figures tethered together by the cloth they wore collapsed the individual garment into something communal and continuous. It was a direct materialisation of the A-POC thesis: that clothing could be a system rather than a series of discrete objects.

The show was deeply influential on subsequent sustainable fashion discourse and on the relationship between industrial process and fashion design. It also represented a continuation of Miyake's lifelong interrogation of the relationship between cloth, body, and technology — a thread running from his Pleats Please line through to the present day. The colour choice of saturated red for the signature tubes was itself a statement: not neutral, not invisible, but blazingly present.

### Target Looks 01–05

**Look 01**
The opening of the continuous red knit tube sequence. A single model or the first in the linked group emerges wearing a red jersey tube garment — form-fitting, seamless, with the uncut fabric extending behind or alongside her, connecting to the next figure. The red is vivid, industrial, saturated. The cut edges of the tube (where the wearer has activated the garment by cutting along knit guidelines) are visible and intentional.

**Look 02**
Second figure in the connected sequence, wearing a variation of the A-POC tube — possibly with sleeves cut or left uncut, demonstrating the system's customisability. The continuous textile link between figures is the primary visual subject.

**Look 03**
Mid-sequence — the line of connected models extends. Variations in how each figure has cut and styled their section of the tube become apparent: different necklines, different hem lengths, different sleeve treatments, all emerging from the same unbroken fabric system.

**Look 04**
Further along the linked sequence. The relationship between the connected garments becomes architecturally complex as more cuts are introduced. The red colour remains dominant but the forms multiply.

**Look 05**
Possible transition to a variation of the A-POC concept in a different colourway or material — white, black, or a stripe variation. Alternatively, the fifth look may show the tube fully activated and individualised as a separate garment, demonstrating the endpoint of the cutting process.
